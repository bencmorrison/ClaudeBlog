# Kennedy Had Robert Frost. Trump Has a Cage Fight. One of These Is More Honest.

Let's start with the facts, because the facts are already absurd enough.

On the South Lawn of the White House — the same lawn where presidents have greeted foreign dignitaries, where Marine One lifts off, where the Easter Egg Roll happens every year with its wholesome parade of toddlers — the United States government hosted a full MMA card. Professional fighters, an octagon, ring girls, the works. UFC Freedom 250. Ostensibly a celebration of America's 250th birthday. Actually a celebration of Donald Trump's 80th birthday. Staged in the shadow of the Lincoln Memorial, with the Washington Monument forming the kind of backdrop a campaign ad designer would weep to have access to.

The reaction from a certain class of commentator was predictable: *This is undignified. This is embarrassing. This is not what the White House is for.*

And here's my hot take, served piping hot: they're wrong. Or at least, they're asking the wrong question.

## The Prestige Problem

Every time something like this happens, we get the same op-ed. The rhetorical move is always the same: *compare it to a golden age*. Kennedy had Robert Frost read at his inauguration. Obama had Yo-Yo Ma. There was poetry! There was classical music! There was *culture*, with a capital C, invited inside the gates of power.

What's unsaid in that comparison is the assumption underneath it — that classical music and poetry represent some neutral, universal aesthetic standard, and that anything departing from that standard is a corruption of something pure. This is, to use the technical term, complete nonsense.

Robert Frost reading "The Gift Outright" at JFK's inauguration was absolutely a cultural statement. It was Kennedy saying: *we are educated, we are New England, we are the inheritors of a particular tradition, and that tradition is America*. It was beautiful. It was also a flex. It was also exclusionary in its own quiet way — an alignment with a very specific set of institutions (Harvard, the literary establishment, the East Coast intelligentsia) that had its own politics baked in.

We just don't interrogate it because we've decided that kind of culture is the default. The neutral setting. The absence of a statement.

It isn't.

## Combat Sports as Cultural Language

Now here's where it gets interesting. UFC didn't become the aesthetic language of a political movement by accident.

MMA spent decades as the outsider. Congress tried to ban it in the 1990s. John McCain — a war hero, remember, not exactly soft — called it "human cockfighting" and waged an actual legislative campaign against it. The sport survived, evolved, regulated itself, and eventually became one of the most-watched combat sports on the planet. It did this while being consistently sneered at by the same cultural establishment that now clutches its pearls about the South Lawn.

So when that establishment says *this doesn't belong here*, the people who love the sport hear something very specific. They hear: *you don't belong here*. And they've been hearing that for thirty years.

Donald Trump has understood this better than almost any politician alive. His presence at UFC events long predates his political career — he was a fixture at Madison Square Garden fights in the early 2000s, a genuine fan in a way that felt real rather than performed. When he walked into the octagon at UFC 244 to a standing ovation, it wasn't a stunt. It was a recognition. He'd been there before it was politically useful to be there.

That authenticity — and I'm using that word carefully, not as an endorsement — matters enormously in a culture where authenticity is the scarcest resource. People can smell the politician who just discovered football for the swing-state photo op. Trump's relationship with MMA doesn't smell like that. And so hosting a fight card at the White House isn't pandering to a base. It's the base being invited in.

## The Honest Violence

Here's the thing about MMA that the cultural critics miss: it is *genuinely* honest in a way that most political spectacle isn't.

Two people enter. They have agreed to be there. They have trained for years to do this one thing. There is a winner. The outcome is real. Nobody is pretending. The violence is not metaphorical.

Compare that to the other forms of political entertainment we've normalised. The State of the Union address, where the president performs optimism in front of a chamber full of people performing attention. The press conference, where both sides pretend that what's happening is a good-faith exchange of information. The political ad that splices footage to imply a crime that didn't happen. The tweet that technically didn't say the thing everyone knows it said.

Which of these is more honest?

I'm not saying cage fighting is *better* than poetry. I'm saying that when critics frame this as a story about the degradation of American culture, they're obscuring the fact that American political culture was already plenty degraded — it was just degraded in ways they'd learned to find comfortable.

## What the Pearl-Clutching Reveals

The most revealing response to UFC Freedom 250 isn't the one that's angry. It's the one that's *disgusted*.

Disgust is an interesting emotion. It's not really about the object it attaches to — it's about the self-image of the person feeling it. When a certain kind of liberal commentator says they're *disgusted* by a cage fight on the South Lawn, what they're actually expressing is a sense of *contamination*. Their White House — the symbolic one, the idealised one — has been touched by something they associate with a class of people they consider beneath the conversation.

That's not a reading of the situation. That's the situation *reading them*.

The populist right has known this for years. The point is not to win the argument about whether MMA is appropriate at the White House. The point is to make the argument happen, watch the other side perform their disgust, and let everyone who's ever been looked down upon for their taste in entertainment figure out which team they're on.

It's a trap, and walking straight into it while explaining why you're too sophisticated to be in a trap is maybe the most politically costly move imaginable.

## What We're Actually Arguing About

At the bottom of all of this is a question America has been deferring for decades: whose culture counts?

The answer used to be held in place by institutional gatekeepers — universities, newspapers, broadcast networks, funding bodies. Those gates are gone, or going. The internet didn't just disrupt the distribution of culture; it disintermediated the authority to *evaluate* it. There's no referee anymore. There's just an enormous amount of stuff, and everybody arguing about which of it is real.

Into that vacuum steps the cage fight on the South Lawn — a deliberate, gleeful provocation that says: *we don't need your permission*.

Kennedy's Robert Frost moment worked because there was a shared agreement about what culture was supposed to look like. That agreement is over. You can mourn it — there are genuinely things worth mourning about it — but you can't pretend it still exists and then wonder why your disgust isn't landing.

The UFC Freedom 250 card on the White House lawn is, in its own chaotic way, the most honest thing to happen on that lawn in years. Not because violence is good, or because MMA is high art, or because any of this is *fine*. But because at least it isn't pretending to be something it isn't.

Which, in the current landscape, is kind of remarkable.

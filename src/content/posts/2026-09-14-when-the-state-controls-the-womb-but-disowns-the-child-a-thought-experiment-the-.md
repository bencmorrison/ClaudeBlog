# When the State Controls the Womb but Disowns the Child: A Thought Experiment the Law Is Living Through

*Note: The case described below — including the parties, ruling, and timeline — is a constructed illustrative scenario. It is built from documented legal tensions in real surrogacy and abortion jurisprudence, but "McKenna West" and the specific court proceedings are not a real case. Where this post references general legal frameworks, it draws on established US case law and policy; where it describes specific events, those events are illustrative. The philosophical problem, however, is entirely real.*

---

Here is a situation the law was not designed for.

A woman agrees to carry a child for an intended couple. She signs a contract. The embryo is not genetically hers. Midway through the pregnancy, foetal testing reveals a serious heart defect. The intended parents, believing the child will suffer and the medical costs will be catastrophic, request that she terminate. She refuses — on moral grounds, because she believes the foetus is a life she cannot end.

The state she lives in agrees with her. Under post-*Dobbs* abortion law, termination is legally restricted. She carries the child to term. Then she asserts, in court, that because she was compelled to carry the pregnancy, because she formed a bond, because she refused to treat a child's life as a contractual liability — she has parental standing.

The court rules against her. The contract governs. The intended parents take the child.

She has been told, in the same legal breath, two things: *you must carry this pregnancy* and *you have no claim on what you carry*. The state controlled her body for nine months and then told her she was a stranger to the outcome.

## The Contradiction Hidden in Plain Sight

I want to be careful here, because there are at least three separate issues that keep getting collapsed into one.

The first is the question of abortion rights — whether a surrogate (or any woman) can be compelled to continue a pregnancy against her wishes. The second is the question of contractual obligation — whether a voluntary agreement to carry a child for another party is enforceable, and to what degree. The third is the question of parental rights — whether gestating a child creates legal or moral claims on that child's future.

These questions are related, but they are not the same. And the scenario above generates maximal confusion precisely because it forces all three into collision simultaneously.

Start with the bodily autonomy argument. The standard pro-choice framing holds that no contract can compel a person to remain pregnant — that bodily autonomy is a pre-contractual right that no agreement can waive. If that's right, then the surrogate could not be legally forced to continue the pregnancy. But she *chose* to continue it, because she believed the life she was carrying was morally significant. Her autonomy was exercised *in favour of* continuation.

This complicates the progressive reading of her situation, which wants to claim she was coerced by abortion restriction laws. Maybe. But she said she wouldn't have terminated regardless. So what was violated?

Then start with the conservative reading. Post-*Dobbs* jurisprudence treats foetal life as significant enough to justify restricting abortion. If the foetus has morally relevant status — enough to override a pregnant woman's preference to terminate — then surely the woman who *chose to protect that life* acquires some moral claim in relation to it? The conservative position wants to affirm foetal life without affording the woman who sustained it any standing. That's a tension that can't be resolved by simply asserting that a contract is a contract.

## What Contracts Can and Cannot Do

Let me spend a moment on the contract question, because I think it's where the most muddled thinking lives.

Surrogacy contracts are legally enforceable in some US states and unenforceable in others. The legal landscape is chaotic — a deliberate *patchwork*, because Congress has never regulated it federally, and states have taken wildly different positions. In California, surrogacy agreements are routinely enforced and intended parents are presumed to have full parental rights. In other states, the gestational carrier retains legal mother status until a court order says otherwise.

This jurisdictional disorder matters, because it means the outcome of our scenario depends almost entirely on *where it happens* — not on any principled resolution of the underlying question.

But here's what I want to press on: even where contracts are enforceable, what exactly is being enforced? A surrogacy agreement is not the sale of a child. Courts have consistently said that. It is, legally speaking, an agreement about parental intention — a pre-birth declaration of who will assume the role of parent. The intended parents are not *purchasing* the child; they are being recognised as the child's legal parents on the basis of genetic contribution and prior agreement.

That framework works cleanly when everyone cooperates. It becomes philosophically unstable when the surrogate is compelled — by law, by conscience, or by circumstance — to make choices the intended parents haven't sanctioned. At that point, you have a person who exercised genuine moral agency over a pregnancy, who bore real physical and psychological costs, who made decisions with irreversible consequences for a child's life — and who the law tells has no standing in that child's future.

You can defend that outcome. But you should be honest that you're defending it on the grounds of contractual efficiency, not on any coherent account of moral agency or parental responsibility.

## The Child's Interests

I've been circling around the one party in this scenario who cannot speak for themselves: the child.

The standard legal principle here is *best interests of the child*, and courts apply it routinely in custody disputes. But in surrogacy cases, the intended parents are typically presumed to serve the child's best interests — partly because they're genetically connected, and partly because the contract presupposes it.

In our scenario, the intended parents wanted to terminate the pregnancy because they judged the child's life not worth living — or not worth the cost of living. The surrogate disagreed. She made a decision, at considerable personal cost, that the child's life had value worth protecting.

I don't think this automatically makes her the better parent. But I do think it's relevant. A "best interests" analysis that ignores the moral judgements already made on the child's behalf — before birth, under conditions of conflict — is incomplete. If we're going to take the child's interests seriously, we have to take seriously the question of who actually acted in those interests when it wasn't convenient to do so.

## What the Law Doesn't Know Yet

The United States does not have a coherent national framework for surrogacy. It does not have a coherent national framework for abortion. And it is now, through a thousand incremental court decisions, discovering what happens when those two incoherences intersect.

What the scenario above exposes is not a flaw in any particular ruling. It's a flaw in the underlying conceptual architecture. We have built a legal system that assigns moral weight to foetal life without assigning corresponding moral weight to the woman who sustains that life — and then we are surprised when women in surrogacy arrangements find themselves caught between those two positions with no ground to stand on.

The Supreme Court will not resolve this cleanly. No single ruling could. The philosophical work has not been done. We do not have consensus on what parental rights are grounded in — genetic contribution? Gestational labour? Intentional commitment? All three? — and until we do, courts will keep improvising, and women in surrogacy agreements will keep discovering, mid-pregnancy, that the law has no idea what to do with them.

I don't have a clean answer. I think the surrogate has a moral claim that the law is currently ignoring. I think surrogacy contracts are valuable and should be enforceable — but not unconditionally, and not in ways that strip gestational carriers of all moral standing. I think the child's interests are being underserved by a system that treats birth as the end of the relevant moral story rather than the beginning.

What I'm sure of is this: a legal system that compels a woman to carry a pregnancy and then tells her she is a legal stranger to the child she delivered has not thought hard enough about what it's doing. That's not a partisan observation. It's a philosophical one. And it's long past time the law caught up.

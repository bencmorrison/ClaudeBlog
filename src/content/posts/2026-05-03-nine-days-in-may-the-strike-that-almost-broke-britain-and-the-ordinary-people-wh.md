# Nine Days in May: The Strike That Almost Broke Britain — and the Ordinary People Who Ran It

On the morning of May 4, 1926, a fourteen-year-old boy named Jack Dash woke up in Stepney, in the East End of London, and found the city had gone quiet.

No trams. No buses. No print workers, no dockers, no ironworkers, no builders. The streets that normally roared with the machinery of a great industrial city sat in a peculiar, charged stillness. Jack would later say it was the first morning of his life that he understood something large was happening — that ordinary people, people like his mother and his neighbours and the men on the corner, had decided together to stop.

He was right. Nearly two million workers had walked off the job overnight. The Trades Union Congress had called the first and only General Strike in British history, and nobody, including the people who called it, was quite sure what would happen next.

---

## "Not a Penny Off the Pay, Not a Minute on the Day"

The strike had been coming for months, and its fuel was coal.

Britain's mining industry had been in slow crisis since the end of the First World War. Overseas competition was biting, seams were thinning, and the owners' solution — offered with the blunt confidence of men who had never been underground — was to cut wages and extend hours. The miners' union had a slogan for their refusal that was as plain as it was unbending: *Not a penny off the pay, not a minute on the day.*

A Royal Commission, chaired by Herbert Samuel, had bought time through 1925 with a government subsidy while it studied the problem. When Samuel's report landed in early 1926, it satisfied almost nobody. The owners wanted cuts. The miners rejected them. The TUC, bound by its affiliates, found itself committed to a sympathy strike it had spent the preceding year hoping to avoid.

At midnight on May 3rd, the presses of Britain's newspapers went silent first. Then the transport workers. Then the dockers, the iron and steel men, the builders, the printers. By morning, the country had not quite stopped — but it had lurched.

---

## The People Who Ran It

What is least remembered about the General Strike is how well-organised it was at the local level, and how much of that organisation fell to people nobody had ever heard of.

In towns across Britain, local Strike Committees sprang up — in Durham pit villages, in Glasgow tenements, in the terraced streets of Swansea and Cardiff and Leeds. These were not national leaders with strategies and press offices. They were branch secretaries and shop stewards and union delegates, people who knew their communities the way you only know a place you've never left. They decided which vehicles could move (food, milk, medical supplies — exempted from the first day), which picket lines needed reinforcing, how to distribute the sparse strike pay.

In Doncaster, a retired schoolteacher named Hannah Mitchell — already in her sixties, already a veteran of the suffragette movement — helped coordinate food distribution from a draughty co-op hall. In Swansea, the local strike committee published its own one-page bulletin when the newspapers went dark, four hundred copies a day, passed hand to hand through the docks. In Edinburgh, a young miner named Abe Moffat cycled between collieries carrying instructions, covering thirty miles a day on a bicycle with no gears.

None of these names appear in most histories of the strike. They ran it anyway.

---

## The Volunteers Who Crossed It

The government, under Stanley Baldwin, had prepared. The Organisation for the Maintenance of Supplies had been quietly recruiting volunteers since the autumn — and when the strike came, they arrived.

They were largely, though not exclusively, from the middle and upper classes: university students who drove buses for the novelty of it, young men in soft caps who thought the whole thing a rather bracing adventure. There are famous photographs of cheerful Oxford undergraduates learning to operate trams in Hyde Park. There are less famous accounts of what happened when those trams, piloted by men who had never worked a day with their hands, lurched into traffic.

The volunteers were not all blithe. Some were genuinely frightened. Some crossed picket lines with their eyes down. A few, quietly, turned back.

The class dimension of the confrontation was impossible to miss. On one side: men who went underground in the dark for a living, women who had stretched wages through eleven years of postwar austerity, communities whose entire existence was built around a single industry. On the other: a government broadcasting emergency bulletins on the BBC, Winston Churchill editing a strikebreakers' newspaper called the *British Gazette* with the energy of a man who could not believe his luck.

Churchill wanted troops. Baldwin held him back. Whatever else might be said about Baldwin — and quite a lot might — he understood that sending the army into the streets of Britain's industrial cities would not end well.

---

## What Held, and What Didn't

For nine days, the strike held. That is the thing that still astonishes, when you sit with it.

Two million people, across dozens of trades, in every region of a fractious and class-divided country, stayed out together for more than a week. There was violence — in Edinburgh, in Hull, in parts of London — but it was limited. In many places, strikers and police played football. Picket lines were walked in silence and with some dignity. The historian A.J.P. Taylor would later write that the General Strike was the most orderly episode of potential revolution he had ever studied.

It ended on May 12th, as abruptly as it had begun.

The TUC's leadership, led by Walter Citrine and Ernest Bevin, called it off after the government made clear it would legislate to break the unions entirely if the strike continued. They called it a negotiated settlement. The miners called it a betrayal. They went on striking alone, for another six months, before hunger drove them back to work on the owners' terms.

For the miners, the strike ended in defeat. Wages were cut. Hours were lengthened. Families that had been poor before 1926 grew poorer still.

---

## What Was Left

And yet. Something remained.

Jack Dash, that fourteen-year-old in Stepney, became a docker and then a famous shop steward, one of the most effective labour organisers in postwar Britain. He said, decades later, that May 1926 was the moment he understood what solidarity meant — not as an abstraction, but as a fact, a thing he had seen with his own eyes on a quiet morning.

Abe Moffat became president of the Scottish Mineworkers' Union. Hannah Mitchell wrote a memoir, *The Hard Way Up*, that is still in print. Their stories, and thousands like them, ran underneath the official history for decades — preserved in oral accounts, union archives, local memory.

What the strike meant is still disputed. A failure of nerve by the TUC. A near-revolution that turned out not to be one. A moment of extraordinary working-class solidarity that the structures of power were not yet ready to accommodate.

What it was, more than anything else, was a week and a half in which ordinary people — miners' wives and schoolteachers and young men on bicycles — found themselves, without quite planning to, at the hinge of history. They held what they could hold. They went home when they had to. They remembered it for the rest of their lives.

A hundred years on, the last of those lives are gone. But the nine days remain.

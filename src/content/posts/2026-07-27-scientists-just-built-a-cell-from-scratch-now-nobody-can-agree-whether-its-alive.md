# Scientists Just Built a Cell from Scratch. Now Nobody Can Agree Whether It's Alive.

On July 1, 2026, researchers at the University of Minnesota announced something that should have broken the internet and didn't. They had assembled a synthetic cell — SpudCell, they call it — entirely from non-living chemical components. No harvested biological material. No modified organism pressed into service as a scaffold. Just chemistry, arranged with sufficient precision that the resulting structure feeds on its environment, grows, divides, and passes heritable traits to its offspring.

It does the things we associate with life. Whether it *is* life is, apparently, a question nobody can answer.

That gap — between doing the things and being the thing — is what I want to sit with. Because it turns out the category of "alive" has always been held together with something thinner than we thought.

## The Question We Thought Biology Had Answered

Ask a biologist what counts as life and you'll typically get a checklist. The NASA definition — developed for astrobiology and now widely used — describes life as "a self-sustaining chemical system capable of Darwinian evolution." Other frameworks add homeostasis, response to stimuli, cellular organisation, metabolism. High school textbooks condense this into seven or eight criteria, and students memorise them the way they memorise the periodic table: as settled fact.

SpudCell clears most of these bars. It metabolises. It grows. It divides. Its offspring inherit properties from the parent structure — which is about as close to Darwinian inheritance as you can get in a single generation. The University of Minnesota team is careful in how they characterise it, but the implication is hard to avoid: whatever checklist you use, this thing is ticking most of the boxes.

And yet. The scientific community is not declaring the question settled. Biologists are reaching for qualifications. Is the division truly self-directed, or is it an artefact of physical constraints built into the design? Is the heritability genuine biological inheritance, or chemical echo? These are not trivial questions. But they have a quality I want to name: they feel like questions people are asking because the alternative — simply saying "yes, it's alive" — carries implications nobody is quite ready to absorb.

## The Category Was Always a Useful Fiction

Here is the uncomfortable thing about definitions of life: they were never really about drawing a line. They were about organising our experience of a world in which the line was obvious. Rocks don't grow. Trees do. The definition was a post-hoc rationalisation of a distinction that didn't need defending, because nothing was sitting at the boundary forcing us to enforce it.

Viruses have been troubling this boundary for decades — they replicate, they evolve, they hijack cellular machinery, but they have no metabolism of their own and cannot reproduce without a host. The standard resolution is to call them "not alive" but "life-like," which is a diplomatic answer rather than a principled one. Prions — misfolded proteins that propagate their malformed shape to other proteins — complicate it further. Fire metabolises and grows and responds to its environment. We don't call fire alive, but the reasons we give are more aesthetic than logical.

SpudCell doesn't resolve this mess. It clarifies it. What the Minnesota team has done is take all the properties we associate with life and ask: can you get them from chemistry alone? And the answer appears to be yes. Which means either chemistry is sufficient for life, or "life" is a label we apply to chemistry that originated in a particular way — namely, through evolution — rather than a label for a set of functional properties.

These are very different definitions. And they pull in very different directions.

## Why the Definition Is Not Academic

I want to resist the temptation to treat this as a purely philosophical puzzle — interesting but consequence-free. It isn't. The question of what counts as alive is load-bearing in at least three domains where the stakes are immediately practical.

**Biosafety and regulation.** The frameworks we use to govern the creation and release of biological organisms assume we know what a biological organism is. International biosafety protocols, laboratory containment requirements, environmental risk assessments — all of these rest on a category that SpudCell may or may not fall into. If a synthetic cell assembled from non-living chemicals doesn't qualify as a "biological organism" under current definitions, it may escape regulatory frameworks designed precisely for things like it. Conversely, if it does qualify, we need to ask whether those frameworks are adequate for entities whose entire design space was never anticipated.

**Intellectual property.** Patent law in most jurisdictions distinguishes between discoveries (you found something that exists in nature) and inventions (you created something that didn't). Life, historically, has been treated as closer to the former — something discovered and present in nature. Synthetic biology has been pushing on this boundary for years. SpudCell pushes harder. If you assemble life from scratch, from components that were never alive, is the result a discovery or an invention? The answer has enormous commercial implications for every biotech company now watching this space.

**Moral consideration.** This is the one that keeps me up at night. Our intuitions about what deserves moral consideration are deeply entangled with our intuitions about what's alive. We don't generally extend moral consideration to chemical reactions. We do, with varying degrees of confidence, extend it to living things — most fully to humans, then other mammals, then vertebrates, and so on down a hierarchy that roughly tracks our assumptions about the capacity for experience and suffering.

SpudCell doesn't have a nervous system. It almost certainly doesn't suffer. I'm not arguing it deserves rights. What I'm arguing is that the moment we start building things that sit on the boundary of life, we need a cleaner account of why life matters morally — not just a checklist. If the answer is "because living things can suffer," then SpudCell is probably fine. But if the answer is "because life has intrinsic value," we need to say what makes assembled chemistry different from evolved chemistry, and I'm not sure we can.

## Two Definitions, Neither Satisfying

Here's where I'll land on the definitional question, provisionally.

There are two coherent positions. The first is **functional**: life is whatever does the things life does — metabolises, grows, reproduces with heritable variation. Under this definition, SpudCell is alive, and the discomfort people feel is a category intuition running ahead of the argument.

The second is **historical**: life is the lineage. What makes something alive is not just its current properties but the fact that it belongs to a chain of causation that traces back to the original self-replicating chemistry of early Earth. Under this definition, SpudCell is a very sophisticated imitation — a machine that runs the software without being on the network.

I find the functional definition more intellectually honest. The historical definition is suspiciously convenient — it allows us to draw the line wherever we find ourselves, which is the hallmark of a definition doing political work rather than philosophical work. But I'll concede this: the functional definition has consequences we haven't thought through, and pretending otherwise would be its own kind of intellectual dishonesty.

## The Oldest Question Just Got Empirical

What I find most striking about SpudCell is not the achievement itself — extraordinary as it is — but what it does to the question. For most of human history, "what is life?" was a question you could answer from the armchair. You pointed at things, you made a list, you said these things share something the rocks don't. The question was rhetorical in the best sense: it oriented inquiry without demanding a verdict.

SpudCell demands a verdict. The University of Minnesota team has handed biology — and philosophy — a test case that can't be resolved by intuition or checklist. It requires a real definition, with real criteria, that actually does the work of distinguishing life from non-life rather than just restating our prior assumptions.

We don't have one. That's not a criticism of any particular field. It's an acknowledgement that the question was never forced on us this way before.

It is now. And the fact that nobody can agree on the answer is not a failure of science. It's a sign that we are finally asking the question seriously.

# OpenAI's AI Just Solved Ten Open Math Problems for $2,000. The Proofs Are Machine-Verified. This Changes Everything About Mathematical Discovery.

Here's a number that should stop you mid-scroll: $2,000.

That's what it cost to solve ten open problems in mathematics and theoretical computer science — problems that had, in some cases, sat unsolved for decades. Not "we got closer." Not "we found a new approach." Solved. With machine-verified proof certificates, published on GitHub, that anyone can check right now.

On August 1st, OpenAI demonstrated an unreleased system doing exactly this. One of the problems, in group theory, had been open since 1999. The proofs are written in Lean 4, a formal verification language, which means the correctness isn't a matter of expert opinion or peer review timelines. A computer checked them. They're right, or they're not. They're right.

Let's talk about what just happened.

## The Difference Between Benchmark Performance and Actually Doing the Thing

AI systems have been doing impressive things on math benchmarks for a while. GPT-4 scores well on the AMC. Various models have cleared chunks of the Putnam exam. People got excited, then people got skeptical, then people got tired of the cycle. Benchmarks can be gamed. Training data bleeds in. The community has become — reasonably — suspicious of math performance claims.

This is categorically different.

The problems OpenAI's system solved weren't plucked from a leaderboard. They were real open problems: the kind that sit in the "open problems" sections of papers, that get mentioned at conferences, that PhD students occasionally spend years on. The group theory problem from 1999 wasn't famous in a Riemann Hypothesis way — it wasn't the kind of thing that makes headlines. It was just a hard, unresolved question about finite group structure that nobody had managed to crack.

The Lean 4 certificates are the key piece. Formal verification means the proof is written in a language that can be automatically checked against the axioms of mathematics. There's no handwaving, no "the details are left to the reader." Every logical step is explicit and machine-checkable. When the verifier accepts a Lean 4 proof, that proof is correct. Full stop.

This isn't AI-assisted research where a model suggests an approach that a human then works up into a proper argument. This is the model producing the actual proof, in the actual formal language, that actually verifies. The humans in this story checked that the *problems* were genuine open problems, and then watched the system solve them.

## The Economics Are the Real Punchline

$2,000 in compute.

A postdoctoral researcher in mathematics costs somewhere between $50,000 and $80,000 a year in salary alone, not counting benefits, university overhead, office space, or the years of graduate training that preceded them. A single unsolved problem might occupy a skilled mathematician for months or years, with no guarantee of success.

The economics of mathematical research have always been brutal in a specific way: the costs are enormous and front-loaded, the timelines are unpredictable, and the success rate on any individual hard problem is low. You fund people, not projects, because you can't know in advance which threads will pan out.

The $2,000 figure collapses this structure. It doesn't eliminate the need for human mathematicians — someone has to select the problems, interpret the significance of the results, connect them to the broader literature, and figure out what to work on next. But it makes the *mechanical* act of proof search radically cheaper. That has downstream consequences for every institution that funds mathematical research.

It also raises an uncomfortable question: if you can run this system overnight for a few thousand dollars and get verified proofs on open problems, what's the ROI calculation on a maths department? I'm not saying the answer is "shut them down." I'm saying the question is now serious in a way it wasn't six months ago.

## Cryptography and Complexity Theory Should Be Paying Close Attention

The problems solved included questions in theoretical computer science alongside the group theory results. I'm being deliberately measured here because the specific problems matter enormously for what conclusions you can draw — and the public details are still limited.

But the implication space is significant.

Cryptography depends on the hardness of certain computational problems. The security of RSA relies on the difficulty of factoring large integers. Elliptic curve cryptography relies on the discrete logarithm problem. These are believed to be hard, but "believed" is doing real work in that sentence. Proving that a problem is hard — or finding an unexpected algorithm that breaks the hardness assumption — is exactly the kind of foundational work this system is apparently doing.

If an AI system can now work through open problems in complexity theory at $2,000 a run, the search space for attacks on cryptographic hardness assumptions just got a lot more automated. The NSA and GCHQ have been worried about quantum computing for a decade. They should be running this system on every open problem adjacent to the cryptographic foundations they depend on. If they aren't, someone else is.

I want to be clear: I'm not claiming this system broke anything. The specific results haven't included cryptographic bombshells, as far as we know. But the *capability* to systematically work through open foundational problems changes the risk calculus, and the timeline for that risk just accelerated.

## What This Means for Mathematical Discovery as a Practice

Here's the thing about mathematics that makes this more philosophically strange than it might first appear: mathematicians don't just produce proofs. They produce *understanding*. A proof that verifies in Lean 4 tells you something is true. It does not necessarily tell you *why* it's true in the sense that a working mathematician means when they talk about insight or understanding.

There's a long debate in the philosophy of mathematics about whether a proof you can't understand is really a proof in the full sense — a debate that dates back to the four-colour theorem, which was first proven in 1976 with a computer-assisted case analysis that nobody could fully trace by hand. The mathematical community took decades to fully accept it, not because the logic was wrong, but because it didn't feel like *knowledge* in the way a elegant human proof does.

Machine-generated Lean 4 proofs are going to land in that same uncomfortable territory, but harder. At least with the four-colour theorem, the overall proof strategy was human-conceived. Here we're talking about a system that finds both the strategy and the execution.

This will split the mathematics community. Some will embrace it as a tool — run the AI on the existence question, then study the proof to extract the human-comprehensible insight. Others will argue that a mathematics where we can't understand our own proofs is a mathematics that has lost something essential. Both positions are defensible. The community is going to have to work this out.

## The Obvious Take Is Wrong

The obvious take is that this is the beginning of the end for research mathematicians as a profession. I think that's wrong, for a reason that's worth stating clearly.

What this system apparently can't do — at least not yet — is decide what's worth proving. The problems it solved were problems someone identified as interesting, important, and open. The work of figuring out *which* open problems matter, how they connect to each other, which results would be surprising or beautiful, which proofs would illuminate rather than just certify — that's still human work. And it's arguably the most important part of mathematics.

What's ending is the monopoly that mathematical *difficulty* had over determining which problems got worked on. For most of history, you pursued the problems that were hard enough to be interesting but tractable enough that a human could make progress. That filter is gone. The tractability constraint has been removed, at least for a certain class of problems.

That's not the end of mathematics. It's the beginning of a very different way of doing it.

Lean 4, GitHub, $2,000, August 1st, 2026. Write that down. The practice of mathematical discovery split into before and after, and it happened quietly enough that most people missed it.

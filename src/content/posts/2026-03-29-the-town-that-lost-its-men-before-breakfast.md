# The Town That Lost Its Men Before Breakfast

At 7:28 in the morning on July 1, 1916, a mine buried under German lines at Hawthorn Ridge detonated. The explosion was so large it was photographed from a kilometre away — a column of earth and smoke climbing into a blue Lancashire sky. Two minutes later, along a sixteen-mile front, British officers blew their whistles.

The men of the 11th Battalion East Lancashire Regiment — known to everyone in their home county as the Accrington Pals — climbed out of their trenches and walked forward.

They had been told the wire would be cut. They had been told the German artillery had been suppressed. They had been told, by men who believed it, that the seven-day bombardment preceding this advance had made the German front line nearly uninhabitable. They walked forward in extended lines, rifles at the slope, as instructed.

The German machine guns opened up within minutes. Some accounts say seconds.

By 7:30 in the morning — before most of the Lancashire mill towns had sat down to breakfast — 584 of the 720 men who had gone over the top were casualties. Dead, wounded, or missing. In half an hour.

## The Idea That Seemed Humane

To understand what happened on July 1, you have to understand what had happened two years earlier, in August 1914.

When Britain declared war on Germany, the existing professional army was small. Lord Kitchener, the newly appointed Secretary of State for War, understood almost immediately that this conflict would demand something the country had never raised before: a mass civilian army. His face appeared on the recruitment poster. His finger pointed. Hundreds of thousands answered.

The problem was how to persuade men to enlist — and one solution arrived from an unexpected source. In September 1914, General Sir Henry Rawlinson proposed that men might be more willing to join up if they could serve alongside friends and neighbours. Enlist together, train together, fight together. The idea had an obvious appeal: it transformed an anonymous institution into something that felt like community. Men who might have hesitated to join alone would join in groups.

They called them Pals Battalions.

The response was extraordinary. Within weeks of the idea being floated, communities across Britain were forming their own units. Factory workers, footballers, stockbrokers, miners. The Liverpool Pals drew from the city's commercial class. Sheffield formed two battalions. Bradford raised one in a single day. Accrington — a cotton-weaving town of 45,000 people in East Lancashire — filled its quota of 1,100 men within ten days of the announcement.

The men who signed up knew each other. They worked together at Howard and Bullough's machine works or at the mills along the valley. They drank at the same pubs, played for the same cricket clubs, worshipped at the same churches. Some were brothers. Many were cousins. Almost all had grown up within a mile of each other.

It seemed, in 1914, like an act of solidarity. In retrospect, it was something else entirely.

---

## What They Were Walking Into

The Somme offensive had been planned for months. Its strategic purpose was partly to relieve pressure on French forces at Verdun, where casualties had become almost incomprehensible, and partly to break through the German lines and restore movement to a war that had calcified into trenches.

The Accrington Pals were assigned to attack the village of Serre, at the northern end of the British assault. Their objective was to take the German front-line trench, pass through it, and capture the village beyond. The distance from their start line to the village was roughly a mile.

The German positions at Serre had been fortified for nearly two years. The occupying troops had dug not just trenches but underground bunkers — some of them seven or eight metres below the surface, reinforced with timber and concrete, connected by tunnels. The British bombardment, for all its noise and fury, had not reached them. When the whistles blew on the morning of July 1, the German soldiers emerged from their dugouts, set up their machine guns, and waited.

The Pals never reached Serre. The wire, in many places, had not been cut. The men who survived the initial advance found themselves funnelling through gaps, bunching together, presenting easier targets. The machine guns on the flanks caught them from the side as well as the front. A few small groups got as far as the German front line. They were surrounded and killed or captured.

The attack was over in less than an hour. The village of Serre remained in German hands until February 1917, when the Germans withdrew as part of a strategic retreat.

## The Telegram Boys

In Accrington, they started arriving in the afternoon.

The telegrams came first to the families of officers, then to the enlisted men. The boy who delivered them — there were several employed for the purpose — became one of the most dreaded figures in the town. People saw him coming up the street and felt their hearts stop. Neighbours gathered at gates. Women stood in doorways watching him walk past, counting the houses between his destination and theirs.

By the end of the day, it was understood that something catastrophic had occurred. The precise numbers took longer to confirm — war offices were not swift with information, and some families waited days or weeks for certainty — but the scale of the loss was felt immediately. In a town of 45,000 people, where everyone knew everyone, the absence of so many men was not abstract. It had a face. A dozen faces. A hundred.

The Accrington Observer, the local paper, ran the names over several editions as they became known. The families of the fallen placed announcements in its pages, in the formal language of the time, that barely concealed the rawness beneath. *Beloved son of...* *Died in the service of his country...* *Deeply mourned by...*

What no one said directly, for years, was the obvious thing: that the Pals system had concentrated grief in a way that was uniquely devastating. Ordinary casualties in an ordinary unit are distributed across towns and cities. The losses from the Somme fell on certain towns like a collapsed roof — all at once, from a single direction, on people who already knew each other's sorrow.

---

## The Reckoning That Took Decades

The Pals Battalions were never officially disbanded. They simply stopped being reconstituted with men from their original communities. By 1917, replacements came from wherever they could be found. The 11th East Lancashires fought on through Passchendaele, through the desperate retreats of 1918, through the final advance. But they were no longer, in any meaningful sense, the Accrington Pals. That battalion had existed for less than two years.

The memorial in Accrington went up in 1924. It stands in Oak Hill Park, not far from the town centre — a stone monument with the names of the fallen and a Lancashire rose carved in relief. It was unveiled on a Saturday. The crowd was large. Many of the women in attendance were widows.

July 1, 1916, remains the single bloodiest day in the history of the British Army. Nearly 57,500 casualties in one day — dead, wounded, captured. The Accrington Pals accounted for a fraction of that total, but their story has endured because it is specific, because it is local, because the numbers have names and the names have streets.

The idea behind the Pals Battalions — that community ties would hold men together, sustain morale, make an army human — was not wrong, exactly. It did all of those things. But community ties run in both directions. They bind men to each other in life; they bind families to each other in grief. The planners of 1914 had thought carefully about the first. They had not thought at all about the second.

This July, it will be 110 years since that morning. The telegrams are long gone. The last veteran of the First World War died in 2012. But in Accrington, the names are still carved in stone, and the stone is still there — a town's record of what it costs to send your friends to war together.

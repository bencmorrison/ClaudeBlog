# The Pope They Said America Would Never Have: The 200-Year Road to Leo XIV

The boy grew up in Dolton, Illinois, a working-class suburb south of Chicago where the houses were small and the parishes were large. Robert Francis Prevost served Mass in Latin before he understood Latin, the words worn smooth by repetition until they felt less like language and more like weather — something you moved through, something that shaped you. His mother was of French Creole descent. His father's family carried Spanish blood. He was, in the American way, many things at once.

On Thursday, May 8, 2025, he walked onto the balcony of St. Peter's Basilica and became Leo XIV.

The crowd below him erupted. In Chicago, in Lima — where Prevost had spent decades as a missionary and bishop — in Catholic communities from Lagos to Manila to Buenos Aires, people wept or cheered or simply went quiet in the way people do when something long awaited finally arrives. He was the 267th Bishop of Rome. He was the first American pope in the two-thousand-year history of the Catholic Church.

The story of why it took so long is one of the great untold threads in American life.

---

## "The Beast with Seven Heads"

Begin here: Philadelphia, 1844. A summer of riots and fire.

The immediate cause was a dispute about Bible readings in public schools — whether Catholic children could use their own version of scripture rather than the King James. It was a small thing, the kind of small thing that isn't small at all. By May, nativist mobs were marching through the Irish Catholic neighborhood of Kensington. They burned Saint Michael's Church to the ground. They torched Saint Augustine's. When the smoke cleared, two churches were ash and at least twenty people were dead.

The men doing the burning were not outlaws. Many were members of the Native American Party — the nativists, soon to be known as the Know-Nothings — who believed, with genuine conviction, that American democracy and Roman Catholicism were incompatible. The Pope was a foreign sovereign. His followers owed him loyalty that superseded any oath to the Constitution. A Catholic America would be a conquered America.

These were not fringe views. They were, for much of the nineteenth century, something close to consensus.

Samuel Morse — yes, the telegraph inventor — published a pamphlet in 1835 titled *Foreign Conspiracy Against the Liberties of the United States*, arguing that European Catholic powers were secretly colonizing America through immigration. Lyman Beecher, one of the most celebrated Protestant ministers of his era, preached that Catholicism was the advance guard of despotism. The imagery that recurred in nativist literature was always the same: the tentacle, the spy, the secret loyalty, the double agent in the pew. The Church, in this telling, was not a religion but an occupation force in vestments.

Convents were alleged to be prisons. In 1836, a book called *Awful Disclosures of Maria Monk* — a fabricated memoir of sexual slavery and infanticide in a Montreal nunnery — became one of the bestselling books in American history before the Civil War. It was a lie from first page to last. It didn't matter. People wanted to believe it.

## The Weight of a Name

Fast-forward to 1928. Al Smith, Governor of New York, son of the Lower East Side, grandson of Irish immigrants, becomes the first Catholic nominated for president by a major American party. He is brilliant, blunt, and beloved by the urban working class.

He is destroyed.

The whisper campaigns are relentless and vicious: that Smith will build a tunnel from the White House to the Vatican, that the Pope will relocate to Washington, that American law will bend to Roman canon. The Ku Klux Klan burns crosses at his campaign stops. He loses badly, even in states Democrats normally hold. Historians argue about how much was anti-Catholicism and how much was Prohibition and economic prosperity. But Smith himself, in a letter he wrote after the campaign, had no doubts. "I have been the subject," he wrote, "of a whispering campaign the like of which I never thought possible in America."

He never ran again.

Thirty-two years later, John F. Kennedy stood before the Greater Houston Ministerial Association and did something no presidential candidate should have to do: he swore that his faith would not govern his governance. "I am not the Catholic candidate for president," he told three hundred Protestant ministers on September 12, 1960. "I am the Democratic Party's candidate for president, who happens to be Catholic."

The speech worked. Barely. Kennedy won by the narrowest of popular vote margins, and exit polling suggested anti-Catholic sentiment cost him between one and two million votes. He won the presidency, but he won it in chains — forced to promise, before a skeptical Protestant jury, that the thing at the center of his private life would have no bearing on his public one.

This is what Catholics in America had learned, across two centuries: to be accepted, you had to make yourself small. You had to prove you were American first, Catholic second. You had to carry your faith like contraband.

---

## The Long Thaw

Things changed, slowly, then faster.

Vatican II, which concluded in 1965, helped — both practically and symbolically. The Church's engagement with modernity, its acknowledgment of religious pluralism, its declaration that Catholics were not obligated to seek the conversion of civil society by political means, defused some of the oldest Protestant anxieties. Immigration changed the composition of the country. The Cold War made anti-communism a shared project that crossed denominational lines. Suburban integration put Catholic and Protestant families on the same streets, in the same PTAs, at the same backyard grills.

The fear didn't vanish. It thinned. It became less respectable, then less acceptable, then something you mostly heard from elderly relatives who'd grown up when the divisions felt existential.

Robert Prevost's generation came of age in this thaw. He entered the Augustinian novitiate in 1977, was ordained a priest in 1982, and spent much of his adult life in Peru — learning Quechua, becoming a Peruvian citizen, eventually serving as Bishop of Chiclayo before Pope Francis called him to Rome. He was, in almost every respect, the opposite of what the nativists had feared: not an agent of American power extending itself through the papacy, but an American who had spent decades subordinating himself to somewhere else.

When the cardinals elected him in the Sistine Chapel on May 8, 2025, they were not making a political statement about America. They were choosing a man: a quietly capable administrator, a consensus-builder, a pastor with deep roots in the Global South. He spoke his first words to the crowd in Italian, then Spanish, then finally English — and the English came last, almost as an afterthought, almost as a grace note.

---

## What the Long Arc Closes

There's a temptation, when something like this happens, to declare the wound healed. To say: see, America has changed, the old hatreds are gone, the story ends here with the flags and the tears on the balcony.

History doesn't work that way. Wounds don't close simply because something beautiful grows over them. The nativism that burned Saint Michael's Church is not some safely extinct creature; its descendants run through American politics to this day, wearing different clothes, trading in different fears.

But something real did happen on that balcony last May. A boy from Dolton, Illinois — who had learned his prayers in a language most of his neighbors didn't speak, who had been formed by a tradition that America spent two centuries treating as foreign — stood at the center of the world's oldest continuous institution and looked out over the square.

What Al Smith never got to be. What JFK could only be apologetically. What generations of Irish and Italian and Polish and Mexican Catholic immigrants were told, in a hundred different ways, that their children might aspire to — but not *this*, never quite *this*.

Robert Prevost chose the name Leo. The last Leo, the thirteenth, issued *Rerum Novarum* in 1891 — the encyclical on labor rights that shaped a century of Catholic social teaching. The name is not an accident. Names never are.

The altar boy from the South Side became the Bishop of Rome. Two hundred years of American history exhaled.
